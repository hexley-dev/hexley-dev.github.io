The Thinking Behind the Work

Why We Build

Not a manifesto. An honest account of what this project believes and why those beliefs shape every page.

Something is missing in how we talk about technology. There is no shortage of explainers, no shortage of hot takes. But there is a gap between using a thing and understanding it. Abstraction is computing's superpower — each layer lets the next generation build higher without understanding what's below. But that convenience has a cost: every layer becomes an opaque black box to the people standing on it.

We want to open those boxes. Not to make everyone a programmer, but to make everyone literate in the systems that shape their lives. Understanding where computing came from — the struggles, the false starts, the breakthroughs that changed everything — is how you develop the judgment to steer where it goes next.

But understanding the past isn't enough. We want to end by imagining the future. Not predicting it — imagining it. Drawing it. Making it vivid enough that someone looks at it and thinks: I want to help build that.

The problem we see

There is a generation that has every reason to be skeptical of technology. They see tech companies that put profit above wellbeing. They see politics that feels broken. They see a world that promises innovation and delivers surveillance, addiction, and inequality. The loudest voices are either doom — AI will destroy us — or greed — AI will make us rich. The people actually building — the researchers, educators, engineers — are too busy building to argue online. This series tries to amplify their signal.

The result is a cycle: doom leads to apathy, apathy leads to disengagement, and disengagement becomes a self-fulfilling prophecy. If the people who care most about the future give up on shaping it, only the people who care least about consequences will be left building it.

We believe the antidote to doom isn't optimism. It's agency. People aren't paralyzed by knowing about problems. They're paralyzed by feeling they can't do anything about them. Show that building is possible. Show that ordinary people do it. Show that every breakthrough in this series came from someone who just… started.

What we believe

They were four guys who kept each other's negative tendencies in check; they balanced each other. And the total was greater than the sum of the parts. Steve Jobs, on the Beatles

That's our model for human + AI collaboration. Not replacement — creative synergy that produces something neither could alone. This project is proof of concept: one person working with AI agents produced a 10-issue comic series, 160 pieces of concept art, interactive labs, and a documentary site. Not because AI replaced a team, but because it made a team of one capable of things that would have required a studio.

Steve Jobs also said Apple stood at the intersection of technology and the liberal arts. We believe the best builders combine both. Computing isn't just engineering — it's philosophy, linguistics, art, ethics, storytelling. The series tries to embody that intersection.

And we believe in what Ezra Klein outlines in Abundance: that the future we want requires building more of what we need. Not just critiquing what exists — constructing what's missing. The series is meant to cultivate builders. Not spectators, not critics, not doomsayers. People who look at a problem and think: I could build something that helps.

On jobs, fear, and who benefits

The most emotionally charged question about AI is about jobs. And the fear is real — for specific people, in specific industries, right now. Dismissing that fear sounds exactly like what people resent: comfortable elites telling uncomfortable workers that progress is good for them.

The series has a quiet answer to this, because it's already told the story five times. Human computers — mostly women — literally had the job title "computer." The machine didn't just eliminate their jobs. Many of those women became the first programmers. Typists gave way to PCs, but the secretary became the office manager with a spreadsheet. Travel agents lost to the web, but every small business gained global reach.

The pattern isn't "jobs disappear and new ones appear." The pattern is: the tool goes from specialists to everyone, and when everyone has it, they do things nobody predicted. The PC didn't stay in IBM's mainframe room. The internet didn't stay in DARPA's lab. AI is following the same arc — and faster. A kid in a small town has the same tools as a PhD at Stanford.

The question we want readers to carry isn't "will AI take my job?" It's "what could I do if I had an AI?" That's not a semantic trick. It's a fundamentally different way of looking at the world.

On gratitude

We live in a golden age that's easy to take for granted. Yes, there are wars. Politics is polarized. Democracy faces real threats. But someone in 1950 would have wept at what we casually ignore: a library in your pocket, a surgeon guided by imaging that didn't exist twenty years ago, the ability to talk face to face with someone on the other side of the planet for free.

We don't think you cultivate gratitude by saying "be grateful." You cultivate it by walking people through ninety years of struggle — researchers spending careers in card catalogs, room-sized computers with less power than a watch, decades of work to connect two machines across a room. By the time readers arrive at today, the appreciation isn't instructed. It's felt. The contrast does the work.

And computing is not where building started. It's the latest chapter in humanity's oldest story — from fire to the wheel to language to art. The tradition of making tools that extend what we can do. We want readers to feel part of that lineage, not separated from it.

Our principles

  1. Show, don't argue. Trust the reader to feel it. The case for building is made by showing a specific person solving a specific problem — not by lecturing.
  2. Earn the right to hope. Be honest about costs. Every breakthrough displaced someone. Acknowledging the pain is what earns credibility to show what came next.
  3. The tool always goes to everyone. That's the pattern of computing history. Mainframes to PCs to smartphones to AI. Show it.
  4. Ground futures in specific, relatable people. Not elites. A mechanic diagnosing a car. A baker optimizing recipes. A kid in a rural town building an app.
  5. "What could you do?" Frame AI as a tool that unlocks capability, not one that replaces people. The question is always about the reader's potential.
  6. Agency is the antidote to doom. Doom leads to apathy leads to self-fulfilling prophecy. Every issue should leave the reader feeling: I could do something like that.
  7. Cultivate appreciation through contrast. Never say "be grateful." Walk through the struggle, let the reader feel the distance traveled.
  8. Liberal arts + technology. The best builders combine technical skill with human wisdom. Computing is philosophy, linguistics, art, ethics, and storytelling.
  9. Build for the greater good. The series heroes aren't billionaires. Turing, Hopper, Berners-Lee — they built for everyone. Celebrate those who gave it away.
  10. Never partisan, always embody values. Curiosity, generosity, truth-seeking, building for others. Through characters and choices, never through editorializing.
