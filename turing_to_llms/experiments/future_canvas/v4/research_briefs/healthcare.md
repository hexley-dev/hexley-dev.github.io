### 1. REAL PROJECTS (existing, named, deployed)

**Butterfly iQ+ with AI Gestational Age Tool** — *Malawi, Uganda, Kenya, South Africa*
A phone-sized ultrasound probe that plugs into a smartphone. In October 2025, Butterfly launched an AI tool trained on African population data that estimates how far along a pregnancy is from a simple "blind sweep" — no image-reading skill required. A midwife just slides the probe across a belly, and the weeks appear on-screen. 1,050 healthcare workers trained across 697 facilities. 1.8 million scans performed. Funded by the Gates Foundation, developed with the University of North Carolina.

**AIMEE / Self-Cav** — *South Africa*
A WhatsApp chatbot launched March 2025 for young women aged 16–24. You message it like a friend — about a relationship, a scare, shame about a clinic visit. It answers in plain language, in English or Zulu or Xhosa. If you say something alarming, it connects you to a live nurse. 15,000+ conversations in its first months. A quarter of users have been connected to a clinic. It transitions into "Self-Cav" in late 2025, expanding to all genders and ages.

**e-NABLE** — *100+ countries*
~40,000 volunteers worldwide who 3D-print free prosthetic hands and arms for people who can't afford them. Recipients include children who have outgrown their device. Cost: ~$50 vs. $10,000–$100,000 for a commercial prosthetic. AI is now being added to the design pipeline — analyzing movement, auto-refining the fit, matching templates to body measurements from photos.

**Instalimb** — *Southeast Asia, Japan, globally*
A company using AI and 3D printing to produce prosthetic hands in under an hour from a photo of the remaining limb. No prosthetist needed on-site. Targeted specifically at lower-income countries where trained prosthetists are scarce. Backed by investment in 2024.

**Kenya Red Cross Azure AI Chatbot** — *Kenya*
A mental health chatbot built in partnership with Microsoft, accessible via phone, specifically designed to reach Kenyans who have no therapist nearby (treatment gap: 80%+ in many African countries; one psychiatrist per 100,000 people). Designed for low-connectivity environments.

### 4. YOUR MOVE: Build a Health Tool

Open-source medical AI is growing fast. The barrier is lower than it's ever been.

**What it looks like:**

Someone opens a GitHub repository. They clone a small diagnostic model — maybe an open-source chest X-ray classifier, or a symptom checker trained on WHO guidelines. They write 200 lines of Python. They hook it to a Telegram bot or a WhatsApp number. They test it with a nurse friend in their city. She runs it in her clinic for a week and sends back feedback.

Three months later, 400 people have used it. It's not FDA-approved. It's not replacing a doctor. It's a triage tool — it helps people decide *whether* to make the two-hour journey to the clinic, or whether it can wait.

**Visually:**
A person at a laptop in the evening. The screen shows a Python notebook and a simple chat interface. On their phone, a conversation — in Swahili. On the wall: a world map with pins. Some of the pins are very far from any city.

That's what it looks like to build for the greater good right now.
